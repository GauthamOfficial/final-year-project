Yala National Park — Sri Lanka's Premier Wildlife Reserve
=========================================================

Yala National Park spans 979 square kilometres across the Hambantota and Monaragala districts in southeastern Sri Lanka. It is the country's second-largest national park and, by visitor numbers, its most popular wildlife destination.

Wildlife Highlights
-------------------

Yala is best known for hosting one of the highest densities of wild leopards (Panthera pardus kotiya) found anywhere on earth. Block 1, the park's most-visited zone, contains an estimated one leopard per square kilometre. Other resident mammals include Sri Lankan elephants, sloth bears, sambar deer, spotted deer, wild boar, water buffalo, and grey langurs. Over 215 bird species have been recorded inside the park, including painted storks, peacocks, and the migratory yellow-footed green pigeon.

Best Time to Visit
------------------

The optimal window is February through July, when water levels in the park's interior tanks (man-made reservoirs) drop and animals concentrate around the few remaining water sources, dramatically improving sighting probability. The northeast monsoon (October–January) brings heavy rain, fills the tanks, and disperses wildlife; Block 1 is closed for restoration each September.

Tour Logistics
--------------

Most visitors enter via the Palatupana gate near Tissamaharama. Half-day jeep safaris (4-5 hours, departing pre-dawn or mid-afternoon) cost approximately LKR 12,000–18,000 per jeep plus park entry fees. Hiring a licensed naturalist guide significantly improves chances of leopard and elephant sightings; ask your hotel or tour operator to confirm the guide's accreditation.

Safety and Conservation
-----------------------

Standing in the open jeep is forbidden in zones where leopards are active — a small number of fatal attacks have occurred when tourists ignored this rule. Plastic bags are banned inside the park; bring a refillable bottle and a reusable bag for any waste.

Yala is administered by the Department of Wildlife Conservation, which uses ticket revenue to fund anti-poaching patrols and habitat restoration after the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami devastated parts of the coast.
